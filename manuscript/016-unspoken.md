# Chapter 16: Unspoken

By the fourth week, the greenhouse had acquired its own weather.

Condensation gathered on the underside of the grow lamps and fell in slow, fat drops. Warm soil breathed against the carrier’s colder metal. The beans had climbed their first strings, lettuce crowded the shallow trays, and a tray of herbs refused every kindness Ava offered it.

She recorded each failure in the journal Gabriel had given her.

*Basil: too much water.*

*Second sowing: fungus at the roots.*

*Third sowing: Marcus dropped a wrench on it.*

She had just underlined the last entry when Marcus arrived with a crate balanced against his hip.

“Before you accuse me,” he said, “there are no tools in this one.”

Inside were filter mesh, irrigation clamps, and three jars of seeds with their labels worn away.

“Where did you get these?” Ava asked.

“Cora’s private kingdom. She said you could have them if I stopped asking.” He placed the crate safely on the floor. “Gabriel’s team checked in, by the way.”

Ava’s hand stopped above the jars. “You could have started with that.”

“I thought I did.”

Gabriel had been gone for six days on a supply run to two abandoned outposts. Six days was not long by the crew’s measure. It was long enough for Ava to notice the absence of his boots outside the greenhouse, his habit of stealing the bitter leaves she told him were not ready, and the way every unfamiliar engine note made her look toward the docking bay.

“They’re safe?” she asked.

“Safe, irritated, and one axle short of a smooth ride home. Jenna expects them tomorrow.” Marcus watched her turn one of the seed jars in her hands. “You know, you could ask him why he does this.”

“Does what?”

“Leaves places where people need him, finds other places where people need him, then acts surprised that anybody worries.”

Ava set the jar down. “He told me his parents died. Not what happened.”

“Not to me.” Marcus nudged the crate farther from the table edge with his boot. “Might talk to you.”

He left her sorting the irrigation clamps, rehearsing a question she was not sure Gabriel would welcome.

That afternoon Jenna came to inspect the water figures. She read Ava’s columns twice, checked the tank gauge, and frowned at the beans.

“They’re using less than the projections,” Jenna said.

“The projections assumed runoff. I catch it.”

“Of course you do.”

Jenna touched one leaf with the back of a finger. The gesture was so tentative Ava almost pretended not to notice.

“My sister kept a plant in a cooking pot,” Jenna said. “After our mother died, it was the only thing in our quarters she remembered to water.”

Ava waited. Jenna spoke of her sister as Gabriel spoke of the fortress dead: without tense, until the tense became unavoidable.

“The infection took her?”

“Yes.” Jenna removed her hand from the leaf. “I knew the symptoms. I knew the protocol. Knowing did not help as much as I had built my life around believing it would.”

The confession did not ask Ava to forgive the sealed rooms or the hazmat suit. It explained nothing away. Jenna picked up the water ledger and tapped a calculation with one short nail.

“Your reclamation estimate is optimistic.”

“By four percent.”

“Six.”

They argued until it became almost pleasant.

Gabriel returned the following evening with a split lip, a torn glove, and a crate of plumbing seals the carrier actually needed. Ava met him in the vehicle bay before deciding whether she meant to.

He climbed from the transport and saw her between two unloading crews. His exhausted face changed.

“Hey,” he said.

She had imagined this moment often enough to resent every version of it. In none of them had she stood speechless with potting soil on her cheek.

“Your lip is bleeding.”

“Good to see you too.”

“What happened?”

“A cabinet.”

Rhea, passing with a coil of cable, said, “The cabinet was stationary.”

Gabriel took the cable from her and carried it without being asked. “Hostile architecture.”

Ava smiled despite herself. Only when he moved past did she see the fatigue in the set of his shoulders. She reached for the lighter end of the coil.

“I can carry that,” he said.

“I know.”

He let her take it.

Later, they sat between the greenhouse beds with their backs against the water tank. The grow lamps had dimmed to evening levels. Gabriel ate lettuce straight from a tray while Ava slapped his hand away from the stronger plants.

“Marcus says you frightened an axle into breaking,” she said.

“Marcus talks too much.”

“He says you don’t talk enough.”

Gabriel stopped reaching for the lettuce. “About?”

“Why you joined the rescue crews.”

There. Asked. Ava turned the journal pencil between her fingers and resisted the urge to apologize for wanting an answer.

“You can tell me it’s none of my business,” she said.

“It isn’t.”

She nodded once.

“That doesn’t mean I won’t tell you.” He drew up one knee. The torn glove had left a red line across his palm. “My father taught me to fight fire. Mostly that meant teaching me not to. You close a door, clear a building, kill the fuel. Heroics are what you do after the plan fails.”

“Did you work together?”

“For a while. My mother hated both our schedules.” The beginning of a smile passed over his mouth. “When the spores reached our district, I was across the river on a factory call. Quarantine sealed the bridges before I got back.”

Ava set the pencil down.

“I spent two days trying other routes. By the time the cordon opened, there was nobody left to rescue.”

“Gabriel—”

“So I kept going. Different district, different people. There’s always another route to try if you arrive early enough.”

The greenhouse pumps clicked on. Water travelled through the tubes in a series of small knocks.

Ava understood the shape of his kindness then. It was not certainty. It was an old refusal sharpened into a profession.

“My parents never knew what happened to me,” she said. “That hurts more than knowing they’re dead. They might have searched. They might have blamed each other. For them, I never stopped being missing.”

Gabriel did not tell her they would have been proud. He looked down at his injured hand and said, “That’s cruel.”

The plainness of it undid her.

She covered her face. Gabriel moved closer, then stopped before touching her.

“May I?” he asked.

Ava nodded.

His arm settled around her shoulders. His coat smelled of cold air, smoke residue, and the harsh soap from decontamination. She leaned into him until the water tank pressed a chill line down her spine.

“I’m glad you came back,” she said into his shirt.

“I said I would.”

“People say many things.”

“I’m trying to be selective.”

She lifted her head. They were too close for the moment to remain innocent, close enough that she saw the fresh cut inside his lip and the darker ring around his blue irises. Gabriel’s attention dropped to her mouth. Neither moved.

Then a pipe coughed above them and emptied a spoonful of cold condensation down the back of his collar.

Gabriel swore and jerked upright. Ava laughed so hard she had to brace a hand against the floor.

“Your greenhouse attacked me.”

“Hostile architecture.”

He pointed at her as if promising retaliation, but he was laughing too.

The almost-kiss remained between them, altered but not erased.

Over the next several days, Ava found reasons to work near the vehicle bay when Gabriel had a shift. He found reasons to inspect irrigation fittings that had not leaked once. Neither mentioned the water tank.

One night she found him outside the greenhouse door with a mug in each hand.

“Caf?” he offered.

“I thought you liked me.”

“This is how people show affection now. Civilization changed.”

She accepted the mug. Below the carrier, a settlement’s work lights marked a rough square in the snow. Figures crossed between them, unloading medicine and repaired filters from *Haven’s Vanguard*. No grand future waited beyond the glass. There were only people still awake because something needed doing.

Gabriel touched his cup to hers. “To the fourth basil planting.”

“Third.”

“The wrench counts.”

Ava leaned her shoulder against his. “Fine. To the fourth.”

Neither of them stepped away.
