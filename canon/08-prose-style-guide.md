# Prose Style Guide

This guide defines the continuing prose style for *Ava: Sporelight*. It is a craft standard, not a continuity source: story facts still come from the other canon documents.

## The Voice in One Paragraph

Write close to the body and close to the person. The world is vast, altered, and sometimes beautiful, but Ava experiences it through damp soil under a thumbnail, a filter rasping beside her, or the shame of being watched while she eats. Gabriel understands danger through exits, injured people, and what his hands can carry. Let the larger themes—bodily autonomy, fear, love, survival, and chosen responsibility—emerge from such particulars. The prose may become lyrical at moments of awe or intimacy, but it should return quickly to something physical, imperfect, and specific.

## Point of View

- Use close third person, normally one viewpoint per scene.
- Filter description through the viewpoint character’s knowledge, habits, and immediate concern.
- Ava notices growth, contamination, texture, and biological change. Her botanist’s attention should survive her fear.
- Gabriel notices routes, structural hazards, breathing, weight, equipment, and people who need help. His firefighter and rescue training shapes his attention.
- Jenna notices systems, symptoms, risk, and the cost of a decision across the whole crew.
- Do not reveal a distant character’s private thoughts merely to foreshadow a turn. Show what the viewpoint character can observe and misread.

## Human Texture

Characters should not speak as polished summaries of the theme. Give them partial answers, interruptions, practical evasions, badly timed humor, and silence that has a visible cause. A personal voice often comes from the detail a character chooses not to explain.

Prefer:

> Gabriel set the cup beside her untouched tray. “Caf,” he said. “Cora claims the machine used to make coffee.”

Over:

> Gabriel offered her comfort, and for the first time she felt a fragile sense of hope.

The first version lets care appear through an object, a joke, and what both people avoid saying. The second announces the intended emotion.

## Sentence and Paragraph Rhythm

- Keep action sentences short enough that geography and cause remain clear.
- Let reflective passages lengthen, but vary their syntax. Several sentences beginning with a name or pronoun create a generated, report-like cadence.
- Use fragments sparingly for shock, recognition, or a hard turn in thought.
- Start a new paragraph when the speaker, physical action, focal idea, or emotional pressure changes.
- Do not explain the same beat after dialogue has already carried it.
- End scenes on an image, action, or unresolved choice rather than a general statement about hope, darkness, fate, or the future.

## Description

Choose two or three telling details instead of inventorying a room. Favor details that do more than one job: a clogged filter can establish danger, scarcity, and Gabriel’s fatigue at once.

Recurring imagery belongs to this novel when it changes with the story:

- roots and cultivation for identity, care, and persistence;
- cold for suppression, safety, and emotional distance;
- amber light for Ava’s mutation and the cost of survival;
- breath, masks, and filters for vulnerability and trust;
- worn metal and repaired machinery for inherited survival.

Do not repeat an image unchanged. Stars are not automatically freedom; spores are not automatically snow; every corridor does not need to hum. Haven’s Vanguard is an atmospheric hover carrier, so windows show weather, terrain, engine wash, settlements, and the actual night sky—not an orbital void.

## Emotion

Give Ava an interior life as well as an observable response. Close third person may enter her unfinished thoughts, private associations, contradictory wants, and memories; physical detail is not a substitute for that access. Let her sometimes know what she feels and sometimes discover it late. Her grief needs time on the page, including ordinary losses that neither rescue nor romance can repair.

Her womanhood is particular to her: scientific ambition, bodily privacy, vanity, anger, attraction, sexual wanting, and later motherhood may coexist without defining a universal female response. Preserve curiosity, humor, impatience, and pleasure alongside vulnerability. Do not make suffering her only claim on the reader.

Render emotion as a sequence:

1. trigger;
2. involuntary bodily or attentional response;
3. character choice;
4. consequence.

Vary the response according to the person and moment. Avoid relying on stock signals such as a clenched jaw, caught breath, pounding heart, tightened stomach, trembling hands, or an unreadable gaze. These actions are not forbidden, but they must be the most exact observation available, not shorthand used by default.

Do not name an emotion and then restate it through body language. “She was frightened; her hands trembled with fear” spends two sentences on one fact. Let the scene prove the feeling.

## Dialogue

- Give each exchange an immediate purpose: obtain information, avoid a truth, repair harm, test trust, or make a decision.
- Keep exposition under pressure. A character explains only what the listener needs and may interrupt, misunderstand, or challenge it.
- Gabriel is plainspoken and practical. His reassurance is strongest when he offers an action, a limit, or his presence—not a perfect speech.
- Ava can be precise about plants and painfully indirect about herself. As her confidence grows, her sentences should become less apologetic and her decisions more direct.
- Jenna speaks with clinical economy. Warmth appears in what she changes or permits, not through a sudden loss of professionalism.
- Harrow bargains, reframes, and leaves himself exits. He should not sound like a philosopher or mastermind.
- Use dialogue tags mainly for clarity. Let nearby action carry tone when possible.

## Action, Horror, and Mutation

Action reveals priorities. Track who is protected, what route remains open, and what each decision costs. Keep combat spatially readable and remember that ordinary mutant deaths can release spores.

Ava’s transformation is body horror, not a clean power-up. Include pressure under skin, altered balance, heat or hunger, torn clothing, lost time, pain, shame, exhaustion, or fear of enjoying the strength. Gabriel can help her orient herself, but he cannot switch her mutation off. When Ava uses blood or self-injury, show the bodily cost and do not turn it into a limitless effect.

Violence may be brutal. Make it consequential rather than decorative. Aftermath—decontamination, damaged filters, grief, depleted ammunition, an injured relationship—belongs to the action scene.

## Romance and Intimacy

Treat intimacy as character action, not reward. Attraction should change what Ava and Gabriel notice, risk, admit, or refuse. Preserve explicit consent and room for either person to slow or stop. Gabriel’s protectiveness can create conflict; it does not give him authority over Ava. Ava’s trust does not erase trauma.

Use concrete, individual details in intimate scenes: a scar one of them has learned, a nervous joke that fails, the awkward work of removing damaged gear. Avoid generic montages of touches, gasps, racing hearts, or bodies moving in perfect rhythm.

The intended audience is 17+. Tenderness, sexual desire, nudity, and adult sexual intimacy may coexist with awkwardness, anger, humor, and trauma. Do not make every romantic beat a clinical exchange or a lesson about consent; establish choice clearly and let the characters experience one another. Preserve the source’s mature intensity while sharpening its language.

Ava should experience attraction from inside, rather than appearing primarily as the object of Gabriel’s gaze. Give her anticipation, curiosity about him, private embarrassment, pleasure, and the ability to initiate. Intimacy can bring delight without erasing fear; every touch need not trigger a trauma recollection. Carry the relationship into ordinary mornings and field decisions after the confession.

## Worldbuilding

Put rules into use. Show spore danger through a detector reading, a filter check, or a route abandoned after a mutant dies. Show scarcity through repairs, ration decisions, and reused materials. Do not pause the scene for a setting summary that the viewpoint character already knows.

Development datasets, game systems, sequel material, and alternative names remain noncanonical unless promoted through the continuity ledger. Mechanical labels such as player choices, outcomes, statistics, quests, or move names must never appear in publishable prose.

## Cliché and Repetition Pass

An author-supplied phrase blacklist may help diagnose repetition, but the manuscript must remain editable without external development files. It is not an automatic replacement list. During line editing:

1. flag exact matches and near-equivalents;
2. decide whether the line is specific enough to earn its place;
3. replace generic emotional shorthand with character-specific observation or action;
4. remove duplicate reassurance, attraction, exposition, and scene openings;
5. retain intentional thematic echoes only when the surrounding meaning has changed.

Never trade one familiar phrase for another. Often the best revision is deletion.

## Scene-Level Revision Checklist

Before a scene is considered finished, ask:

- Whose experience controls the scene?
- What does that person want right now?
- What concrete detail could only occur in this world and this viewpoint?
- What changes by the end of the scene?
- Is any emotion explained twice?
- Does dialogue sound spoken rather than composed for a synopsis?
- Is the action physically and biologically possible under canon?
- Does Ava make her own consequential choices?
- Could one paragraph be removed without losing meaning?
- Does the final line land on something more specific than a declared theme?

## Final Standard

The desired prose is intimate, tactile, unsentimental, and capable of sudden beauty. It should feel authored by someone who cares about these particular people, not assembled from the expected language of post-apocalyptic romance. Specificity is the main defense: one stained glove, one failed joke, one seedling with a split stem, one choice that costs more than the characters expected.
